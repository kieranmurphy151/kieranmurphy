Medi-Connect Conference - Devon Air Ambulance

I attended the Medi-Connect Conference 2026 hosted by Devon Air Ambulance in Exeter on the 1st of October 2026. The theme for this years conference was "Beyond Boundaries: partnerships in emergency response" The first talk was a case study of an RTC from start to emergency call to bystander actions to the prehospital care to follow up. Some points I found interesting were as follows:

Prof. Tim Nutbeam talked about some of the specific areas of care on this call and discussed some areas investigated by IMPACT:

  • There has been a significant amount of guidelines centered around having TXA administered within 2hrs , however it has been shown to be more beneficial given earlier so it should be given ASAP. Projects looking at empowering bystanders to give IM TXA are underway.
  • He mentioned nasal packs (which we use) and bite blocks for maxfax #'s in terms of an Airway intervention, I didn't know what the bite block's were so researched them after the conference to familirise myself.
  • The current evidence based extrication per the EXIT project (an online course i'm attending at current) specifically he was asked several questions by attendees which were interesting. The first was what exactly do we mean by a 'rapid' extrication and how at present it is not clearly defined and each organisation fire vs ems or even individual clinicians will have differing mental models on what a rapid extrication is. Specifically the fire service may consider cutting the roof off and pulling patient up a hard board from a reclined seat a rapid extrication however when this was measured in practice it takes > 30mins on the other side a 'snatch rescue' which we would consider for cardiac arrest where we break a window and pull the patient from the car. It was suggested that a rapid extrication would be somewhere between those 2. A means of extrication for a patient who is not physically trapped was one team member accesing the patients feet from the passenger side , removing the patients shoes and untangling feet from pedals, the fire service could push the driver door open past its stopper for 180 degree access to driver side and a teardrop barrier from the fire service could be slid under the patient and the patient lifted out on the teardrop onto a waiting board , minimising time patients are trapped and minimising cutting and in escence this is the mindset for any rtc patient, if they can step out they should and if they are unable to step out they need to be out ASAP, not dying in a car while we slowly extricate them. Another question was asked regards what level of monitoring is required for these patients , it was said that monitoring ads 7 mins and delays the fire service, it was suggested that any time critical interventions such as IM TXA or CABC interventions are done and then patient is extricated, it was asked what benefit will knowing the vitals know, especially considering if you determine the patient is crashing you are already extricating the patient as fast as possible but your monitoring is delaying said extrication. Another question was asked regards pelvic binder and it was suggested that in car based RTC's there is a low occurance of pelvic fractures that benefit from a pelvic binder however in the likes of horse riding or motorcycle incidents this is different and a high suspicion should be maintained

Dr David Potter discussed the patients journey in the hospital:

  • specifically the importance of a good ATMIST with time to allow resus to get the pertinent specialties there with the equipment/ blood ready. With a emphasis on how resus should be a pitstop in patients care not a speedbump. He fielded some questions of direct to CT pathways and how sometimes total process optimisation (straight to CT or pre drawn drugs etc) can result in too much momentum being built and no time to review or think or question what is happening right now. Which I found perticulary interesting especially his emphasis on maintaining momentum pre hospitally and balancing interventions vs delaying acess to definitive care. Perfect is the enemy of good enough.

Prof. Tim Nutbeam spoke again on some interesting points :

  • That Interventions should make an immediate difference not just be done because it was historically done
  • Cars collect vast amounts of data from crashes such as duration, weight on seats etc however access is not implemented in the UK
  • Over triage is common
  • It is really difficult to differentiate big sick from little sick in injured patients, stable vitals means nothing
  • Human side of patient care : communication, taking care of them, patient being informed and pain managed, being told they are okay, holding there hand is what patients remember.
  • Bystanders should be thanked and supported post incident

Dr Iona Taylor spoke on the RNLI's interaction with other services and the setup of their vessels and casualty care capabilities.

ACP Chris Knight from the major trauma ward in Plymouth spoke on the major trauma network in the area and some of the challenges:

  • Silver trauma still under triaged for major trauma
  • Discussion as to what patients are major trauma vs not is still common and difficult, advised discussing with clinical seniors pre hospital, or specialist care or consultant service that may be available in future

Hamish Finlay a pilot for Devon Air Ambulance spoke on some of the challenges of HEMS flight :

  • What is bad flying weather? , forecasting is majorily important to day to day operations, there are legal limits , fog, cloud and ice are more restrictive to flying than wind
  • When requesting a heli consider weather at your location doesn't equate to good flying or weather the heli has to get through
  • Landing - HEMS unique with landing in non controlled spaces , size of heli important, day time 2x helicopter lengths space 135 is 14m, at night 2d x 4d
  • 5s recce assesment : size, shape, surround, slope, surface (wheels vs skids) // access for clinical recce , security
  • Hazards - livestock (will kill chickens), people are eejits, wires, loose rubbish, crops, dust, vehicles, other aircraft, people again, tall things, roads have to be closed both directions,
  • Night flying , nvg , different weather limits and landing ( pre surveyed sites ) not absolutely needed, everything slower
  • Can be done not equal to should be done

Medical lessons from the Isle of Man TT racing - Gareth Davies

I attended a live webinar hosted by Trauma Care in which Gareth Davies the medical director of the Isle of Man TT spoke on some of the challenges, solutions, resourcing and specific cases from the Isle of Man TT.
He highlighted the challenges presented to the Isle of Man healthcare system by the large event, from both major trauma to lower acuity presentations due to the large influx of visitors as well as how the nature of the race and closed public roads poses challenges to emergency service response outside of the race. Some of the measures taken to alleviate this are mutual aid, additional resourcing. He also highlighted that all police officers aswell as the fire service are trained to a level of pre hospital care and it would be plausible to arrive to a scene where the police are often the first responder and the patient being ventilated with an Igel as needed due to the training the police receive.
There were several points he made regarding clinical management that I found particularly interesting and are as follows

  • They experience increased levels of silver trauma due to visitors and due to the low thresholds of imaging in silver trauma due to the nature of silver trauma and likelihood of patients being anti-coagulated, there is a considerable amount of incidental findings such as previously undiagnosed cancers or lung nodules found in those presenting with trauma.
  • Simulation plays a massive role in their preparedness in a variety of situations from trackside even to the helipad and the sim is repeated throughout the event
  • Racers in the event experience significant physiological stress with HR of >150 for 2 hours + and temperatures of 40 degrees celclciusus during racing
  • Due to the rapid response times many patients are found in the "hyper acute phase of trauma" , several video's where shown and we were asked to imagine the vitals of those patients , it was highlighted how the priority is to address the apnea or dysventilation and circulation.
  • Impact brain apnea was mentioned and I had not previously learned about this so I will further research
  • Trauma induced secondary cardiac damage was also mentioned and I found this interesting, that haemorrhage can lead to myocyte dysfunction and subsequently myocardial damage .

May 20261 hr

IPERN Human Factors SIG

The Irish Paramedicine Education and Research Network human factors in paramedicine special interest group hosted an online event where 2 researchers Ivan McCann and Samuel Fournier presented different talks . These talks were very interesting, I have been reading a book "Under Pressure : Diving deeper with human factors" by Gareth Lock which examines human factors in scuba diving through several case studies, and seeing the same principles applied to paramedicine was very thought provoking. Some of the key concepts I found intriguing from the IPERN talk where as follows :

  • Human factors (HF) is a vast area of study and has its own professionals who soley work/research in that area, a one day training in HF does not adequately train someone in HF as a whole
  • HF (in medicine) is not only about patient safety, it encompasses a wide variety of systems
  • HF does not attempt to eliminate human error instead it focuses on designing resilient systems
  • HF is made up of organisational factors, physical factors and cognitive factors
  • Its easier to change the design of a system than the behaviour of an individual
  • To properly implement CRM the system must be changed not just behaviours
  • In high acuity low occurrence calls HF system designs can be used to potentially improve practice
  • An example of this was designing newborn resus kits where it is unfolded in the correct order of use to reduce task load in the first critical 60s

Traumatic Cardiac Arrest Masterclass

Presented by Dave Irwin from ECG Hub, this live online class - looked at the traumatic cardiac arrest, the potential causes, treatment from the roadside to hospital and walked through each element of the PHECC CPG in detail.

The main point that I found interesting, and I could see myself going down the same path prior to taking this class, was how the initial steps are so different to a medical arrest, and with how regularly we train for medical cardiac arrest, it would be easy to take the same initial steps when presented with a traumatic arrest, however as was explained and referenced to the CPG the initial arresting of catastrophic or suspected catastrophic bleeding is vital even at the expense of delaying BLS or ALS and while with medical cardiac arrests our standard treatment involves a "stay and play" aproach - it was emphasised how important rapid access to critcal care is (wether that be on the road or in ED). Once specific point that I felt was very useful for my practice - was that in a cardiac arrest it can be easy to get caught up with the need to address airway and breathing , however the initial few minutes the airway bag should remain closed (in a 2 person response) as the first priority is confirming the pt is in arrest , starting compression, getting pads on, and delivering a shock if able, then airway should be addressed. This was referenced to the CPG's and is the process the cpg follows through. There was smaller points about how the initial concentration in a shockable rhythm should be shocks not ALS or an advanced airway, how to check if an SGA is leaking, where to position a kit dump, and to keep the BVM mask after moving to an SGA. It mentioned in arrest an ETCO2 of 1.2+ is a sign of perfusion and if ETco2 is dropping with a good airway to check cpr or lucas placement.

There was an additional point about expected changes based on the draft publication of the ERC 2025 guidelines, one of the potential changes to resus was a vector change after 3 unsuccessful shocks in vfib. This is interesting to me and I will look into the effects of vector change defibrillation on recurrent or refractory VF.

GCS; Roadside to ResusThe Resus Room Podcast

The GCS has been a fundamental part of assessing patients with altered consciousness for over 50 years. You’ll find it in trauma scores, neurology exams and practically every prehospital and ED handover. But here's the thing, is it as reliable and useful as we think?
In this episode, we’ll explore the origins of the scale, what it was designed for and how it’s been used (and maybe misused...) since. We take a look at how reproducible it really is, particularly when different clinicians score the same patient. Spoiler alert: it’s not always as consistent as you might hope!
We’ll also unpack the individual components; eyes, voice, motor and ask if they all carry equal weight, or are some more prognostically useful than others? Because a GCS of 4 isn’t always the same GCS of 4, depending on how you get there…
We’ll be looking at real-world implications, how we make decisions around airway management, imaging, and referral, all based on that one number.
So whether you’re in prehospital care, the ED, or intensive care - stick with us as we try to answer the question: is the GCS still doing what we need it to, or is it time to move on?
